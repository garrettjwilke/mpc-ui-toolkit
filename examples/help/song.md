# Song

Song is learned in some passerines and inherited in others. The organ is the same neighbourhood of the body either way: the syrinx, described on the [anatomy](anatomy.md) page. What changes is the number of muscles, and whether the young bird has to hear an adult of its own kind.

## Two sides

An oscine can run the two sides of the syrinx on different schedules. One side may hold a tone while the other adds a faster figure, or the two may alternate so quickly that the ear hears one continuous line. Nightingales, thrushes, and many warblers do this. A sonogram shows the overlap. A person in a wood just hears a phrase that seems too packed for one throat.

Crows do not sound like nightingales, and they are oscines too. The syrinx can make a harsh caw as easily as a whistle. Ravens add knocks, gurgles, and calls that carry a long way. [Crows, ravens, and jays](corvidae.md) are songbirds in the taxonomic sense even when nobody would call the sound a song. The word oscine refers to the muscles and the learning, not to prettiness.

## Learning

A young oscine has a sensory phase, when it memorizes a tutor, and a motor phase, when it practices. The practice is subsong, quiet and variable, then plastic song, then a crystallized song it will use for the season or for life. Isolate a young bird from its species and the song comes out incomplete or odd. That experiment is the evidence that the song was copied, not fully encoded from hatching.

Dialects follow from learning. Birds in one valley share a phrase that birds over the ridge do not. The species is the same. The local tradition is not. Some species keep learning. Others close the window after the first year. Starlings and mockingbirds copy other species and mechanical sounds. They have no pages in this sample. The mechanism is the oscine one: hear, store, practice, repeat.

Suboscines are the contrast. A tyrant flycatcher raised without a tutor still sings a recognizable song of its species. The [suboscine](suboscines.md) page is that side of the split. The songs can be elaborate. They are just not a culture passed by ear in the oscine way. Hybrid zones and playback experiments are how that was shown. Play a neighbour's song and a territorial male answers. Play the wrong species and he often does not.

## What song is for

Males sing to hold a space and to attract a mate. Females of many species sing too, especially in the tropics, where a pair defends a territory all year and both sexes have a reason to announce it. A dawn chorus is many males at once, when the air is still and sound carries. The chorus is not one species. It is the local oscines stacked in time, each with a phrase the others do not use.

Calls are not songs. A call is short: alarm, contact, begging, flight. A song is the longer, repeated pattern. The same syrinx makes both. A [sparrow](sparrows.md) has a simple song of a few notes and a large set of calls. A wren has a loud song for its size. A swallow twitters in flight. All of those are passerine sounds. A dove's coo is not, and a dove has no page here because it is not in the order.

## How to hear the split

If the bird is copying, improvising, or holding a dialect, you are probably listening to an oscine. If every member of the species sounds nearly the same and the young do not need a tutor, you may be listening to a suboscine. You cannot see the muscles. [Taxonomy](taxonomy.md) is the map, and [oscines](oscines.md) is the group with the learned song. The [New Zealand wrens](wrens.md) sit outside that choice.
