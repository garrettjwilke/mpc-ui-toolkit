# Anatomy

The outside of a passerine is a foot, a bill, and a covering of feathers. The inside feature that matters most for this sample is the syrinx, the voice box. [Song](song.md) is about how that box is used. This page is about where it sits and what else in the body is characteristic. [Description](description.md) is the field view of the same bird.

## The bill

The bill is bone covered by a horn sheath, with nostrils near the base. Passerines do not have a cere like a hawk or a parrot. The shape tracks the diet and can change slightly with the season. A sparrow's bill is deeper in winter, when seeds are the work, and a little finer when insects are being carried to chicks. A crossbill's tips cross, left over right or right over left, so the bird can lever cone scales. A shrike's bill has a notch, and the bird is still a passerine.

The skull has to anchor the muscles that close that bill. Seed crackers have deep skulls. Insect pickers have lighter ones. None of this is unique in birds. What belongs to the order is the combination of this bill with the perching foot and the syrinx, not any single measurement.

## The foot, again, as a machine

[Description](description.md) explains the three-forward, one-back toe arrangement. Anatomically, the tendons that flex the toes run behind the ankle joint. Bend the joint and the tendons tighten. The bird does not have to spend effort holding a twig. The muscles that adjust the grip sit up in the leg, which keeps the foot light.

The back toe has its own claw and its own tendon. In larks it is long. In crows it is strong enough to pin an object while the bill works. Swallows have small legs and small feet, and the same tendons. A swallow's foot is not a failure of the plan. It is the plan reduced, because the bird rarely walks.

## The syrinx

The syrinx sits at the bottom of the trachea, where the airway splits into the two bronchi. In most other birds the sound comes from membranes there, and the control is limited. In oscine passerines a set of muscles attaches around each side. The bird can vary tension, airflow, and which side is sounding. The two sides are not forced to play the same note.

That is why a phrase can contain a rapid alternation that one membrane could not produce alone, and why two tones can overlap. The muscles are the reason oscines are called songbirds in the strict sense. Suboscines have a syrinx too, with fewer muscles, and their songs are usually simpler and inherited. The [New Zealand wrens](wrens.md) are outside both of those groups. [Taxonomy](taxonomy.md) draws the line. [Song](song.md) stays with the sound.

The syrinx is not the larynx. Birds have a larynx, and it does not make the song. Air is metered from the air sacs through the lungs and past the membranes. A bird can sing while it flies because the airflow is arranged that way. A swallow over a field is a passerine using both the wing and the syrinx. A crow calling from a post is the same organ turned to a coarser sound.

## Skeleton and muscle

Passerines have a typical bird skeleton: light bones, a keeled breastbone for the flight muscles, and a fused hand in the wing. There is no set of bones that a person can glance at and name the order, the way the foot can be glanced at on a live bird. The details that systematists use are in the palate, the syrinx muscles, and the sperm, among other places. This sample does not open a page for each of those. The useful division for reading is still the one on the [taxonomy](taxonomy.md) page: wrens, suboscines, and oscines.

The neck is flexible. A passerine can reach the oil gland at the base of the tail and then dress every feather it can touch. Preening is constant. A damaged flight feather stays damaged until the next moult. The oil gland is ordinary bird anatomy, not a passerine invention. It is mentioned here because so much of the bird you see is feather, and the feather is maintained, not just grown.
