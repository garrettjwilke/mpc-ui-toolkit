# Taxonomy

Passeriformes is the order. It has three living groups that matter for this sample: the New Zealand wrens, the suboscines, and the oscines. Families are the next rank down, and there are enough of them that the [family list](families.md) is its own long page. This page is the map. [Origin](origin.md) is why the map looks like this.

## The order

Every bird on the [home](index.md) page is in Passeriformes. Ducks, hawks, owls, herons, woodpeckers, parrots, and pigeons are not. They can perch, sing, or eat seeds. They are other orders. The passerine characters used in classifications are the perching foot, details of the palate and the spermatozoa, and the syrinx. No single field mark replaces the set. In practice a birder uses the foot, the bill, and the knowledge of which families exist.

Carl Linnaeus named the genus Passer in the eighteenth century, and the order took its name from that genus. The [sparrows](sparrows.md) are therefore the namesake, not the ancestor. Putting them at the "bottom" or the "top" of a list is a bookkeeping choice. On the tree they are one oscine family among many.

## Acanthisitti

The New Zealand wrens are a few species in one family, Acanthisittidae. They are passerines, and they are not oscines or suboscines. [New Zealand wrens](wrens.md) has the notes on the living birds and the ones that were lost after people reached the islands. They belong on this page because leaving them out makes the order look like a simple pair of groups. It is a pair, plus this small first branch.

## Tyranni, the suboscines

Suboscines have a simpler syrinx and, as a rule, songs that do not have to be learned. Their diversity is in the New World: flycatchers, antbirds, antpittas, tapaculos, ovenbirds, woodcreepers, cotingas, manakins. The Old World holds broadbills, asities, and pittas. [Suboscines](suboscines.md) is the page. Most of those family names return as unopenable text on the [family list](families.md).

A suboscine is not a failed songbird. The group is old, diverse, and, in a Neotropical forest, often the passerines you actually see. Calling them the "other" passerines only means the sample you grew up with was European or North American, where oscines dominate the field guide.

## Passeri, the oscines

Oscines have the muscled syrinx and the learned song. They are the majority of passerine species. Two large clusters are worth knowing. One is the Corvides, around the crows: jays, shrikes, birds of paradise, vireos, and many Australasian families. The other is the Passerida: larks, swallows, tits, warblers of several families, thrushes, flycatchers of the Old World, finches, sparrows, and the American nine-primaried songbirds. [Oscines](oscines.md) opens that split. From there this sample can reach [crows](corvidae.md), [swallows](swallows.md), and [sparrows](sparrows.md).

[Song](song.md) is the reason the oscine line is drawn where it is. [Anatomy](anatomy.md) is the syrinx those muscles sit on. A classification that ignored the syrinx would still find much the same groups in the DNA. The muscle story and the genetic story agree, which is why the split is used here.

## Families, and names that move

A family is a rank, not a fact of nature in the way a species is. Workers split and lump. Old books have a huge family Muscicapidae for almost every small insect eater, or a huge Sylviidae for almost every warbler. Newer lists break those into several families. The [family list](families.md) follows a fine split so that the page is long. If a name is missing, it may be inside a neighbour. If a name cannot be opened, that is because this sample did not give it a page, not because the birds are unimportant.

The path this sample expects you to try is short to say and long to click: [home](index.md), this page, [oscines](oscines.md), then crows, then swallows. Back returns one step. Home returns to the start and clears the stack.
