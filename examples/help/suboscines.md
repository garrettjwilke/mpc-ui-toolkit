# Suboscines

Suboscines are the passerines called Tyranni. Their syrinx has fewer muscles than an oscine's, and their songs are, as a rule, inborn. A young bird does not need to hear a tutor to produce the species song. That is the practical difference from the [oscines](oscines.md). [Song](song.md) compares the two. [Taxonomy](taxonomy.md) is the map that puts this group next to the oscines and the New Zealand wrens.

## The American centre

Most suboscine species live in Central and South America. Tyrant flycatchers are the largest family: a huge set of insect catchers, from tiny short-tailed birds to the fork-tailed flycatcher with streamers. Antbirds follow army-ant swarms and take the insects the ants flush. Antpittas are plump and terrestrial. Tapaculos skulk. Ovenbirds and woodcreepers build or climb. Cotingas include bellbirds and the umbrellabird. Manakins dance on leks. None of those families has a page in this sample. The names are the content of this section, and the wheel should pass over every one of them.

A lowland forest can be mostly these birds. A visitor who knows only European songbirds will misfile them as "some kind of warbler." Many are not oscines at all. The [origin](origin.md) page is why the Neotropics hold them: this is where that branch diversified.

## The Old World minority

Broadbills, asities, and pittas are suboscines in Africa and Asia. Pittas are plump, colourful, and terrestrial, with a short tail. Broadbills have wide bills and, in several species, bright greens. They are few compared with the American families, and they matter because they show the group is not only a New World phenomenon. A [pitta](pittidae.md) and a [broadbill](eurylaimidae.md) have no pages here.

Sapayoa, a small greenish bird of the Chocó forests, is a New World relative of those Old World suboscines rather than of the tyrant flycatchers. It is the sort of single species that makes the tree look uneven. It also has no page. It is named so the American centre is not treated as the only shape the group can take.

## Songs you do not learn

Play a recording of a suboscine and the territory holder answers if the song is his species and often ignores it if it is not. Raise the young bird without adults and the song still comes. Oscines fail that second test. The difference is not that suboscine songs are short or ugly. Manakins and some flycatchers have sounds that are tricky to describe. The difference is where the pattern came from: inherited, not copied.

Calls and mechanical sounds add to the picture. Manakins snap their wings. Some cotingas boom. The syrinx is not the only instrument. It is still a simpler syrinx, in the sense of fewer intrinsic muscles, than the one on the [anatomy](anatomy.md) page for oscines.

## How this sample treats them

You can read this page and return. You cannot open a flycatcher or an antbird from it. The openable neighbours are [oscines](oscines.md), [taxonomy](taxonomy.md), and [home](index.md). The [New Zealand wrens](wrens.md) are the third branch, not a kind of suboscine.

[Tyrannidae](tyrannidae.md), [Thamnophilidae](thamnophilidae.md), and [Furnariidae](furnariidae.md) are the three large American families a longer sample would have given pages. They are text only, on purpose.
