# Brood parasites

A brood parasite lays in another bird's nest and leaves the raising to the host. The habit has evolved more than once. The famous cuckoos are not passerines. Several passerines do the same thing, and they are the subject of this page. [Nests and eggs](nests.md) is the ordinary cup those eggs land in. [Description](description.md) is the adult host, which is often a perfectly typical songbird.

## Inside the order

Indigobirds and whydahs, family Viduidae, are the clearest passerine case. They are African finch-like birds, related to the waxbills, and they lay in waxbill nests. The chick is raised with the host chicks. It does not evict them. A male indigobird learns the song of his host species, which is how a female finds a male who was raised by the same kind of host she is about to use. That is oscine learning, from the [song](song.md) page, turned to a parasitic life. [Viduidae](viduidae.md) has no separate page. This one covers the habit.

The cuckoo-finch, a weaver relative, parasitizes cisticolas and prinias. Cowbirds, in the American blackbird family, lay in the nests of many small oscines and do not match one host the way an indigobird does. A brown-headed cowbird egg can appear in a warbler nest, a sparrow nest, or a finch nest. The host is a passerine. The parasite is also a passerine. [Cowbirds](molothrus.md) have no page of their own.

Honeyguides are not passerines. They are mentioned so they are not filed here by accident. A honeyguide chick often kills the host young. That is a different order and a harsher method. [Honeyguides](honeyguide.md) cannot be opened from this sample.

## What the host does

Hosts sometimes throw out an odd egg, bury it in the lining, or abandon the nest. They sometimes do not. The egg of a specialist parasite tends to look like the host egg. The egg of a generalist is a compromise that several hosts will accept often enough. The chick's gape, the begging call, and the way it begs are all part of the same match. An indigobird chick resembles a waxbill chick. A cowbird chick is larger and begs louder, and it can outcompete the host young without removing them.

Raising a parasite costs the host a brood, or part of one. The host's own chicks may fledge alongside, or they may starve. Over time, hosts that notice the odd egg leave more offspring. Parasites that match the egg more closely leave more offspring. The result is a continuing match, not a finished one. Different host populations of the same species can differ in how picky they are.

## What this is not

A cuckoo in a reed warbler's nest is the picture in most books. The reed warbler is a passerine. The common cuckoo is not. This page will not treat that pair as a passerine parasite. It is the comparison. The passerine versions are the whydahs, the indigobirds, the cuckoo-finch, and the cowbirds.

A bird that dumps an egg in a neighbour's nest of the same species is a different, milder habit. Some ducks and some songbirds do it. It is not the specialized brood parasitism above, where the species never raises its own young. This sample does not open a page for that milder habit either.
