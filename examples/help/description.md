# Description

A passerine is a small or medium land bird with a foot for gripping and, usually, a song. That sentence hides a huge range. A goldcrest is about the weight of a coin. A common raven is heavier than a lot of hawks. Both perch the same way. Both are in this order. The [anatomy](anatomy.md) page names the parts. This page is about what you see when the bird is sitting in front of you.

## Size

Most passerines are small. Warblers, kinglets, wrens, and many finches are under twenty grams. Thrushes, starlings, and jays are larger. Crows and ravens are the heavy end, and a few Australian birds such as the lyrebird are heavy without being corvids. Size is a poor way to decide that a bird is a passerine. A swallow in the air can look like a swift. A shrike on a post can look like a small hawk. The foot, the bill, and the way the bird hops or runs are better clues.

The [New Zealand wrens](wrens.md) are among the smallest. The [crows](corvidae.md) are among the largest. The order did not start at one of those extremes and march toward the other. Both sizes are later outcomes of the same body plan.

## Feathers

Passerines moult, usually once a year after breeding, and some moult part of the plumage again before the breeding season. A male that looks dull in winter may grow a black bib or a bright breast by spring. Females of many species are plainer. That is not a rule of the whole order. In the birds of paradise, the males carry plumes that make no sense as camouflage, and the females are the brown birds that sit on the nest. Those birds have no page in this sample. The feathers still follow the same growth pattern as a sparrow's.

Colour is produced by pigments and by the structure of the feather. Carotenoids from food make yellows and reds. Melanin makes black, brown, and grey. Blues are usually structural: the feather scatters light. A jay's blue and a swallow's blue back are that kind of colour. Wear also matters. Fresh autumn plumage can hide pale edges that rub off by spring, so the same bird looks brighter without growing a new feather.

Down covers the chicks of many species only thinly. They hatch in a nest and are brooded by a parent. That is different from a duckling, which leaves the nest covered and walking. [Nests and eggs](nests.md) is the page for the chick. This page stops at the outside of the adult.

## The perching foot

Three toes point forward and one points back. The back toe is well developed, not a small stub. When the bird bends its leg, tendons pull the toes closed. A sleeping bird on a twig is locked on by that tendon, which is why a gust does not automatically throw it off. The grip releases when the leg straightens for takeoff.

You can see the arrangement on a wire full of [swallows](swallows.md) or on a [sparrow](sparrows.md) in a hedge. Crows use the same foot to walk, to hold food, and to perch. Treecreepers use it on bark. Larks use it on the ground and have a long claw on the back toe. None of those habits removes the toe plan. Birds that are not passerines and still perch, such as cuckoos, falcons, and pigeons, do not share the whole set of traits that define the order. The foot is the one you can check with your eyes.

## Shape in life

Bills vary more than feet. A finch bill cracks seeds. A warbler bill picks insects from leaves. A swallow bill is short, flat, and wide. A crow bill is a general tool. [Anatomy](anatomy.md) treats the bill as a structure. Here it is enough to say that the bill is not what makes a bird a passerine. A hawfinch and a willow warbler are both passerines. A parrot with a seed bill is not.

Wings vary too. Swallows have long pointed wings. Wrens have short rounded ones. A pipit flies up in a flutter and parachutes down. A raven soars. The order includes almost every flight style a land bird can have, except the flightless divers and the hovering hunters that are not perching birds. A few passerines barely fly. The New Zealand wrens include species that hop more than they fly, and some island wrens that are gone were nearly flightless.

## Where the description stops

Plumage and size will not tell you the family. A brown bird with a thin bill might be a warbler, a wren, a female finch, or a young thrush. The [family list](families.md) shows how many of those groups exist, and how few of them this sample can open. For the birds this sample does open, start from [home](index.md), or go on to [anatomy](anatomy.md) and [nests](nests.md).

[Feathers](feather.md) as a subject, [pigments](pigment.md), and the [goldcrest](goldcrest.md) have no pages. The wheel should not stop on those names.
