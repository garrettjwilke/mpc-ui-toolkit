# Old World sparrows

The order Passeriformes is named for the genus Passer. These are the Old World sparrows, family Passeridae. They are small, seed-eating oscines with a conical bill, and they are not the same birds as the "sparrows" of the Americas. [Oscines](oscines.md) is the group. [Taxonomy](taxonomy.md) is why the namesake is not the ancestor. This page is the family itself.

## The house sparrow

The house sparrow is the bird most people mean. The male has a grey crown, chestnut at the side of the head, and a black bib that is larger in the breeding season. The female is plainer, buff and brown, with a pale stripe behind the eye. They live around buildings, take grain and scraps, and nest in holes, in creepers, and in the structures people put up. The bill is deep enough to crack seeds. In the breeding season both parents carry insects to the young, because a chick needs protein a seed does not provide. [Nests and eggs](nests.md) is that part of the life.

The species followed agriculture and ships. It is native across much of Eurasia and North Africa, and introduced in the Americas, southern Africa, Australia, and New Zealand. An introduced sparrow is still a passerine. It does not become a New World sparrow by living in New York.

## Relatives

The Eurasian tree sparrow is smaller-looking, with a chestnut crown and a black spot on the white cheek, and the sexes are alike. It is more of a farmland and village bird in the west of its range, and a common city bird farther east. Rock sparrows are streakier, with a yellow spot at the throat in the breeding season, and they use stony ground and ruins. The Dead Sea sparrow, the Spanish sparrow, and the Italian sparrow are further members of Passer. Golden sparrows and bush sparrows sit in neighbouring genera in the same family. None of those species has a page. The highlight on this page is for other pages, not for each sparrow.

Snowfinches are high-mountain members of the same family, pale and hardy, living around scree and buildings at altitudes where a house sparrow would not. They make the point that Passeridae is not only the bird on the cafe table.

## Not these sparrows

American sparrows, the birds in Passerellidae, are a different family of oscines. They look similar because a small brown bird that eats seeds ends up with a similar bill and a similar streaked back. The resemblance is convergence. A song sparrow or a white-throated sparrow is a passerine and an oscine, and it is not a member of Passeridae. [New World sparrows](passerellidae.md) have no page here. The name is in the paragraph so the two groups are not silently merged.

Finches are another seed-cracking family, Fringillidae, with a different history. A chaffinch or a goldfinch is not a sparrow in this sense. [Finches](fringillidae.md) are text only. So are [weavers](ploceidae.md), which can look sparrow-like and which build the woven nests this sample mentions elsewhere.

## Why the order has this name

Linnaeus described Passer. Later classifiers needed a name for the perching birds, and they built it from that genus. The house sparrow is common, familiar, and easy to point at. It is a poor picture of the whole order if it is the only picture. A [swallow](swallows.md) does not eat grain. A [crow](corvidae.md) is fifty times heavier. A New Zealand wren is not an oscine. They are all in the order named for this small bird. [Home](index.md) is the wider account. [Description](description.md) is the foot and the size range.
