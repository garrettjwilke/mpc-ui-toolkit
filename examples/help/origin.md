# Origin

Passerines are a southern order that later filled the north. The living sister group to all the other passerines is the New Zealand wrens. The suboscines are concentrated in South America. The oscines diversify richly in Australia and the islands north of it, and from there they spread into Eurasia, Africa, and back into the Americas. [Taxonomy](taxonomy.md) is the classification that follows from that history. This page is the history in outline.

## A southern start

The oldest story that fits the birds we have puts the early passerines on the southern continents when those continents were closer. New Zealand kept a remnant, the Acanthisitti, now a few species and several extinctions. [New Zealand wrens](wrens.md) are not "primitive sparrows." They are the branch that split first. Everything you would call a songbird is on the other branch.

Fossils of small perching birds are easy to miss and hard to name. A tiny bone has to show a passerine character, and many small birds share a size. The useful evidence has been DNA, compared across families, with the geography of the living birds as a check. Families that sit at the base of the oscine tree are Australian. Families that sit at the base of the suboscine tree are South American. That is the pattern this page keeps returning to. It is why [Australia](australia.md) and [South America](south-america.md) are named here even though neither place has a page.

## Two radiations

After the split, the suboscines did most of their branching in South America. Tyrant flycatchers, antbirds, ovenbirds, cotingas, and manakins are the bulk of that radiation. A few lineages reached the Old World. Broadbills and pittas are the ones people meet in Africa and Asia. They are suboscines far from the centre of the group. [Suboscines](suboscines.md) is their page. Most of those family names are text only in this sample.

The oscines did the matching thing in Australasia. Lyrebirds and scrub-birds sit low on that tree. Honeyeaters, fairy-wrens, and pardalotes are part of the old Australian songbird fauna. From that stock, birds moved into Asia. Crows and their relatives, the Corvides, are one great branch. Warblers, swallows, larks, thrushes, finches, and sparrows belong to the other great branch, sometimes called Passerida. [Oscines](oscines.md) sorts those names. [Crows](corvidae.md), [swallows](swallows.md), and [sparrows](sparrows.md) are the three this sample can open.

## Into the north, and back

Once oscines reached the continents of the north, they speciated again. The Americas, which already had a full set of suboscines, gained oscines too: the New World warblers, the blackbirds and orioles, the cardinals, the tanagers, the buntings. Those are not suboscines that learned to sing. They are oscines that arrived. A wood-warbler and an antbird can share a forest and not share a recent ancestor inside the order.

Islands kept doing what islands do. A few colonists became many forms. Hawaii's honeycreepers, now mostly lost, were finches that became every bill shape a passerine can have. Galapagos finches are the famous smaller case, the birds associated with [Charles Darwin](darwin.md). Darwin has no page here. The finches are oscines, and the bill story is the one told in brief on the [anatomy](anatomy.md) page: the bill moves, the foot stays.

## What is still unsettled

Family limits move when a new comparison is published. A group treated as one family may be split, or two families may be merged. The three-way split of the order is more stable than the name of every twig. If a name on the [family list](families.md) is unfamiliar, it may be a split that older books still fold into a larger family. The list is there so the wheel has a long page of names. It is not a claim that every one of those ranks is finished.
